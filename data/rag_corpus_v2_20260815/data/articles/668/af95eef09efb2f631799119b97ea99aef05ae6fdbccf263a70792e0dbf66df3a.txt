The generation that writes TikTok manifestos about billionaire greed and the widening wealth gap has somehow, simultaneously, made the aesthetics of extreme wealth concentration its dominant cultural fantasy. Billions of views accumulate under #OldMoney—polo whites, tweed, country-club stillness, even loafers displacing sneakers—has become one of Gen Z’s most visible style movements, alongside Regencycore ballgowns popularized by shows like Bridgerton and HBO’s The Gilded Age, prairie dresses, and homesteading vlogs. Gen Z has built its escape from modern chaos inside the last era that looked exactly like modern chaos. But the period they’ve chosen to romanticize was defined by the same runaway inequality, technological disruption, and political combustibility driving them to escape in the first place. What they’re actually feeling The psychological term for this is anemoia—coined by coined by writer John Koenig in his Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows, defined simply as “nostalgia for a time you’ve never known.” It’s distinct from ordinary nostalgia, which is rooted in real memory: a genuine callback to something personally experienced. Anemoia is essentially an imagined memory, a longing built from movies, photographs, and secondhand stories rather than lived experience. Researchers describe it as a blend of memory and imagination, where the “past” being missed is really a mental simulation constructed from cultural artifacts rather than personal history. Survey research confirms this isn’t a marginal phenomenon. A nationally representative survey of just over 2,000 U.S. adults conducted by the Human Flourishing Lab found that 68% of Gen Z adults report feeling nostalgic for eras before their lifetime, 73% say they’re drawn to media or styles from those periods; and roughly two-thirds say exploring pre-lived eras helps them cope with stress or anxiety about the future. Separate polling shows Gen Z ranks as the generation most likely to say they’d rather think about the past than the future, with researchers linking this to disrupted early development, wealth inequality, and an unrelenting feed of negative information. The anemoia is documented, widespread, and functionally economic—as much a psychological symptom of the affordability and mobility gap between generations as a fashion sensibility. But anemoia doesn’t point randomly at the past. The question is why Gen Z’s imagined longing has settled so specifically on the early 1900s—a gilded age to rival our own, and a far from perfect world, a fin de siecle before the epic disaster of World War I and the following catastrophe of the stock-market crash of 1929 and the Great Depression. The specific signals Unlike the diffuse Y2K nostalgia of earlier internet cycles, Gen Z’s early-1900s fixation is precise and aesthetic-driven. Bridgerton and The Gilded Age sparked measurable spikes in searches for corsets, empire-waist dresses, and opera gloves. The #OldMoney aesthetic has become a defining Gen Z style movement, with commentators describing it as “a way to reclaim control through appearance” amid a housing crisis and gig economy. Tradwife content and its cottagecore predecessor package early-1900s-coded homemaking — modest floral dresses, sourdough, domestic self-sufficiency, as a rebuke to algorithmic overwhelm. And it’s not purely aesthetic: homesteading has become a genuine lifestyle shift, with young people raising chickens, growing food, and moving toward rural self-sufficiency explicitly modeled on earlier agrarian life. Birdwatching among 16- to 29-year-olds in the UK has surged more than 1,000% since 2018, and interest in mahjong clubs—a game especially popular with American housewives in the early 1900s—is up more than 4,000% in the past year, driven largely by young people. Knitting and crochet have also seen a generational takeover, with 73% of crocheters now between 18 and 34. The common thread is control, texture, and slowness, all qualities Gen Z associates with a world that existed before smartphones, gig work, and platform algorithms flattened daily life into something frictionless and disorienting. Why this specific era The pre-digital pull described above—60% wishing for a time before constant connectivity—points toward an era before the internet, not necessarily this one specifically. What sharpens the focus is a second, parallel pressure: anxiety about AI. Gallup polling from early 2026 found 42% of Gen Z report feeling anxious about AI and 31% report feeling angry about it, with anger up nine percentage points and hopefulness down nine points from the prior year, even as usage has stayed roughly flat. An era before automation and platform economics reads, for a generation with this profile, as a psychological refuge. The homestead and the tradwife kitchen offer something algorithms cannot threaten: tangible, hands-on autonomy. The analog trend is already well established, but that explains the fantasy of escape, not the destination toward a world that vanished a century ago. Perhaps there is a strange familiarity because the early 1900s is the last time the world looked structurally like this one. The Council on Foreign Relations’ Gideon Rose has argued that the 1890s–1900s era “certainly” resembles the present, citing “technological change, economic concentration, and rising inequality; political partisanship, financial corruption, and social turmoil; populism, racism, and xenophobia” as structural through-lines connecting the two periods almost point for point. Wealth concentration during the original Gilded Age had a strikingly small circle at the very top: just four families made up the country’s richest 0.00001% by 1913, controlling 0.85% of national wealth. Economist Gabriel Zucman’s analysis found that today, 18 families occupy that same percentile — including Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, and Bill Gates—holding 1.35% of national wealth, which surpasses the historic Gilded Age peak. More broadly, the top 0.01% of American families, roughly 18,000 households, now hold about 10% of the country’s wealth, versus 9% at the end of the original Gilded Age and just 2% in the late 1970s. The technological revolution then was railroads, telegraphs, and mechanization displacing agricultural and artisan labor; now it’s AI and platform economics unsettling creative and white-collar work. Both eras share a common mechanism: technology outpacing the institutions built to manage its social fallout. Gen Z’s anemoia seems to have settled on the early 1900s because it’s the version of the past that requires the least imagination to inhabit, it’s so eerily similar to the present that it barely needs to be imagined at all. Escaping into a mirror This is where anemoia stops being a cultural curiosity and becomes a diagnostic tool. Gen Z isn’t fleeing toward stability; it’s fleeing toward the aesthetics of stability—the corseted composure of a Bridgerton ballroom, the quiet dignity of “old money” tailoring, the self-sufficiency of the homestead—while unconsciously selecting an era whose underlying economic and political DNA looks almost identical to the one driving them there. The contradiction is sharpest in the “old money” aesthetic itself. Gen Z’s frustration with wealth concentration is well-documented culturally, yet the #OldMoney aesthetic idealizes the visual language of precisely the fortune-building class its politics often criticize. One high-school op-ed put the critique bluntly, calling the trend “ignorant” for idealizing the wealthy and “perpetuating values of consumerism and greed while ignoring the consequences of wealth inequality.” Cultural commentary elsewhere has framed the aesthetic less as hypocrisy than as survival cosplay—a curated stand-in for financial security that feels increasingly out of reach, especially as entry-level jobs demand years of experience and AI reshapes hiring for new graduates. In conversation with Fortune last month, Jessica Lautz, deputy chief economist at the National Association of Realtors, described the doubling-up of younger millennial households—driven by high housing costs—as “an older way of living,” adding that it’s “bringing us back, perhaps, to the early 1900s,” when data shows families doubled up at higher rates based on the availability and cost of housing. That comparison was about a structural shift toward century-old housing-market dynamics, not cultural aesthetics, but it underscores that the early-1900s comparison isn’t only a mood board. In at least one documented case, it’s showing up in actual household-formation data. The early 1900s isn’t chosen because it was better. It’s chosen because it’s recognizable.
