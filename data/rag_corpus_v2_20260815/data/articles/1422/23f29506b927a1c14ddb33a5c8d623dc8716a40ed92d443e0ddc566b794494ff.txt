On New Year's Day 1959, Cuba woke up to a new reality. By then, the island had established itself as a tourism hot spot and cosmopolitan travel destination, thanks to its entertainment, hospitality, and proximity to the US. Before dictator Fulgencio Batista fled Havana and revolutionaries led by Fidel Castro transformed the country into the first communist state in the Western Hemisphere, Cuba's social and economic fabric had already begun fraying. The glamour of Havana hospitality masked political repression, corruption, and sharp divides between the capital and vulnerable rural communities. Today, Cubans on the island struggle with shortages of food, water, fuel, and medicine, as well as recurring power outages. Yet some scenes look remarkably similar to those from the midcentury. Historic buildings dot the island, vintage cars preserve a distinctive streetscape, and tourists still come for its beaches, hospitality, and nightlife. Since 1962, the US has maintained a trade embargo with Cuba, restricting most US commerce aside from limited exceptions for food, medicine, and humanitarian exports. Decades of economic animosity and restricted access to US markets have deterred foreign trade and investment in the country and contributed to the island's economic isolation. On January 29, 2026 — shortly after the US captured Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and blocked Venezuela's oil shipments to Cuba — US President Donald Trump signed an executive order creating a process to impose additional tariffs on imports from countries that directly or indirectly provide oil to Cuba. Recent reforms have begun to open Cuba to more private businesses and imports, and the government has announced broader measures to attract investment. But the authoritarian state remains central to the island's fragile economy, and daily life for most Cubans is far more difficult than the island's nostalgic imagery can suggest. See how life on the island has changed since the 1950s, from flashy tourism centers to vulnerable rural communities. In the 1950s, Cuba was a cosmopolitan tourist destination for foreign travelers. Old Havana drew visitors in the 1950s with its historic architecture, hotels, restaurants, and nightlife. Today, there are visible reminders in Havana of that bygone era. Deteriorated historic buildings and vintage American cars remain part of the daily landscape in Havana, though they reflect decades of repair, scarcity, and preservation efforts. Fulgencio Batista seized power in 1952 and ruled repressively. Batista took control in a 1952 military coup and led a government widely associated with corruption, censorship, and violence against opponents. Even after his 2016 death, Fidel Castro's legacy still shapes Cuba. Cuba remains a one-party state led by President Miguel Díaz-Canel, who is also the top official of the Communist Party. Fidel's brother Raúl Castro stepped down as the party's top official in 2021. The 1950s saw a rapid expansion of Cuba's hospitality infrastructure. The Malecón and nearby Vedado neighborhood became focal points for midcentury hotel, apartment, and entertainment development as the government pursued tourism revenue. Development has continued in Havana's tourist districts, even as historic buildings deteriorate. The capital's skyline now combines historic buildings with newer hotels and high-rises, underscoring the government's continued focus on tourism amid a wider housing and infrastructure crisis. Luxurious hotels like the Riviera attracted tourists from all over the globe. The Hotel Riviera opened on Havana's Malecón in December 1957 and became one of the city's best-known casino hotels. Today, the high-rises remain, but tourism is largely state-run and strained. Cuba nationalized foreign-owned hotels after the revolution, and state enterprises still control much of the industry. Recurring blackouts have disrupted businesses and hotels since 2024, as severe fuel shortages and frequent failures in the country's aging, deteriorating power plants strain the energy grid. American tourists flocked to Cuba in the 1950s. American celebrities were among the many visitors Cuba welcomed in the midcentury, when the US was the island's largest tourism market. Americans cannot travel to Cuba freely today. Tourists from other countries still support the industry. Countries like Canada and Russia are among the top sources of international visitors to Cuba today. The Hotel Nacional de Cuba, which opened in 1930, became a symbol of the island's international prominence. The hotel hosted celebrities, politicians, and organized crime figures in the '50s. The Hotel Nacional remains open as a luxury hotel. It survives alongside food shortages, blackouts, and deteriorating housing that shape daily life for Cubans. In the '50s, golf courses such as the Havana Country Club catered largely to affluent foreign visitors and Cuban elites. Americans often came to Cuba to golf. After the revolution, Castro's government shuttered most of Cuba's golf courses. Castro's government closed or repurposed many of Cuba's courses after 1959, as golf was treated as an elitist sport. For decades, Cubans were also barred from staying in many tourist hotels. Today, few courses remain open, though tourism remains vital to Cuba's economy. Cuba has two operating golf courses, in Varadero and Havana, aimed largely at visitors. A 2008 reform allowed Cubans to stay in tourist hotels for the first time since the revolution, though the cost remains out of reach for many. Havana was synonymous with gambling in the midcentury. Havana was a major casino destination in the 1950s, attracting travelers looking to gamble and organized crime figures looking to profit from the city's tourism boom. The revolutionary government outlawed casinos shortly after taking power, and gambling remains illegal today. "La Bolita," a popular clandestine lottery in Cuba, has survived for nearly 70 years since gambling was outlawed, reflecting the role of the underground economy in daily life. Several restaurants and bars in Havana's tourist center became internationally recognizable. Sloppy Joe's was among the city's most popular bars with American tourists and celebrities in the midcentury. It offered more than 80 cocktails and its own brand of 12-year-old rum. Havana's restaurants and bars are still operating, but daily operations are increasingly difficult. Restaurants like La Vitrola are still open in Havana, keeping the hospitality scene alive amid shrinking tourism numbers. Cabarets like the iconic Tropicana nightclub became symbols of midcentury Cuba. The Tropicana nightclub in Havana has attracted high-profile guests since its 1939 opening, thanks to lavish performances that helped define Havana nightlife. The Tropicana has endured with state support. The iconic Tropicana remains open and continues to stage its signature over-the-top performances for visitors and Cubans who can afford to attend. Outside the capital, life was far less glamorous in the 1950s. Much of eastern Cuba faced poverty, seasonal unemployment, poor roads, and limited access to schools, healthcare, and other public services as the capital flourished. Many rural communities still face disproportionate hardship today. The revolution extended state services beyond Havana, but food, fuel, transport, and power shortages often hit rural areas especially hard. Access to water in the 1950s was limited in many rural regions. By 1952, most rural homes lacked indoor running water, and many also lacked electricity, paved roads, and nearby schools. Today, water shortages have made access unreliable even in urban areas. Residents in cities including Matanzas have faced water disruptions as aging infrastructure and electricity shortages affect pumping and distribution. Education in 1950s Cuba was not equally accessible. By 1953, Cuba had a high literacy rate in Latin America, but rural illiteracy was far higher than in urban centers. Today, state-run education is widespread, though strained by shortages. Cuba's broadly accessible free public school system follows a state-directed curriculum that emphasizes the revolution, socialism, and loyalty to the Communist Party, though teacher shortages, power cuts, transportation problems, and a lack of supplies often disrupt classrooms. Public healthcare in Cuba was unevenly available in the 1950s. In the mid-century, private, public, and charitable hospitals provided modern care to some urban Cubans, but medical services were far less accessible in rural areas. Today, Cuba's universal health system is under intense pressure. Cuba still sends medical personnel abroad through international missions to allied countries, even as hospitals on the island face shortages of medicine, equipment, staff, and reliable power. Midcentury Cuba relied heavily on healthcare imports that did not reach all regions equally. Havana was home to more than half of the country's hospital beds at the time. Isolation from the US pushed Cuba to strengthen its domestic health science. Cuba invested in state-run medical research partly to reduce its dependence on imported medicines and equipment. In 2021, the country developed its own COVID-19 vaccines. Private businesses, including grocery stores, were common in 1950s Cuba. The pre-revolutionary economy included privately owned shops, pharmacies, restaurants, factories, and other small businesses. Recent reforms have expanded legal private business, but state rationing remains essential. The Cuban government began rationing food and basic necessities in the early 1960s. In the midcentury, Cuba was a major importer of US-made cars. Havana streets were full of modern cars of the day in 1949, in part thanks to the island's close commercial relationship with the US. Today, fuel shortages and high vehicle costs have pushed many Cubans toward alternatives. Bicycles, pedicabs, and other forms of transport have become especially important on the island as fuel shortages and unreliable buses make travel more difficult. The import of American cars in the midcentury defined the look of Cuban streets for decades to come. American-made vehicles were especially common on the island before the revolution, which suspended imports of US cars and helped preserve many older vehicles on its roads. Since 2014, newer imported cars have begun to alter Cuba's once stuck-in-time image. Modern Chinese, European, and other imported cars now share the road with classic American vehicles and Soviet-era Ladas, though they remain unaffordable for many Cubans. The cigar industry was central to midcentury Cuba. Cigar-rolling factories employed thousands of workers in Havana, while tobacco grown in western provinces such as Pinar del Río was a major export. Today, the cigar industry remains state-controlled and valuable. The Upmann cigar factory was nationalized after the revolution. Cuba's lively culture drew international interest in the midcentury despite domestic troubles. Dancers in traditional costumes grabbed the attention of visitors in the streets of Havana in 1946. Even with widespread blackouts and hardship, Havana's nightlife remains alive. Despite its hardships, Cuba is still lively today. People continue to gather in downtown clubs and bars even as energy and fuel shortages cause blackouts and difficult conditions.
